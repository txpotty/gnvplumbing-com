    Gas Water Heater Code Compliance in Wilmington, DE

    Fuel gas code covers more of a water heater repair than most people expect. We check for a sediment trap, an approved flex connector and a shutoff valve within reach of the unit. Garage installs in Wilmington, DE need the burner raised 18 inches off the floor unless the heater is listed as vapor resistant, and strapping goes on wherever the local code asks for it. We measure clearances as well.

    Venting and Draft Repair in Wilmington, DE

    Atmospheric tanks rely on hot exhaust rising through a metal flue with an upward slope of at least a quarter inch per foot. A loose, rusted or sagging section lets exhaust spill back into the house. The vent gets repaired. Each joint gets three screws, the connector gets proper clearance from wood and drywall, and we run the heater for several minutes with a smoke test at the draft hood to watch the exhaust go up. We do not leave with the heater spilling.

    Propane Water Heater Repair in Wilmington

    Propane and natural gas heaters look the same but burn at different pressures. A propane tank needs its own orifice and a valve set for roughly 11 inches of water column. Natural gas runs closer to 7 inches, so a heater converted badly, or a regulator that drifted, gives you soot, a yellow flame and a pilot that keeps going out for no obvious reason. A manometer reading tells us the supply pressure. A bad conversion gets corrected with the right parts.

    Bradford White and Rheem Gas Water Heater Repair in Wilmington, DE

    Bradford White and Rheem tanks make up a big share of what we repair in Wilmington, DE. Most Rheem gas units run a White-Rodgers or Honeywell valve with a blinking status LED. Bradford White uses its own sealed combustion design with a lint screen at the base and a thermal switch, and it needs a specific kit when the pilot assembly goes bad. Both kits ride on the truck. Genuine parts keep the warranty intact.

    Repairing Older Gas Water Heaters in Wilmington

    Older tanks with standing pilots and no vapor sensor are still out there in Wilmington, mostly in basements and utility closets. Their parts are simple, and a universal thermocouple fits nearly all of them. We can keep a pre-2003 heater running when the tank is sound, but we also check the draft hood, the flue connector and the gas cock, since those fittings on an old install are often original and corroded. Plenty of older installs never got a sediment trap. We add one during the repair.

    Power Vent Water Heater Repair in Wilmington

    Power vent heaters push exhaust through PVC with a blower on top of the tank. When one quits, it is usually the blower motor, the pressure switch or the hot surface igniter. Condensate pooling in a vent with the wrong slope can also trip the pressure switch, so we check the pipe run all the way to the termination before we sign off on a part. The control's error code is our starting point. Then each part gets a meter test.

    Pilot Light Repair in Wilmington

    The thermocouple is the first thing we test when a pilot will not stay lit. With a millivolt meter, it takes about a minute to see if it is putting out enough. If it reads fine, the next suspects are a clogged pilot orifice, a cracked piezo wire or a thermal cutoff that tripped because the combustion chamber overheated from blocked air. Figure on roughly an hour for most pilot repairs.

    Gas Valve and Thermostat Repair in Wilmington, DE

    Lukewarm showers and a tank that never catches up often trace back to the gas control. The tech checks the dial, the immersion sensor and the outlet pressure with a manometer. On most residential tanks the thermostat lives inside the gas valve body, so a bad sensor or a stuck magnet usually means replacing the whole control rather than one small part. Plan on roughly an hour for the swap. Every joint on the new valve gets leak tested.

    Most gas water heater repairs in Wilmington, DE land between $150 and $800. A thermocouple or pilot assembly sits at the low end, around $150 to $350. A gas control valve replacement usually runs $400 to $800 or more once you add the part, the labor and the leak testing on every joint we disturb. We quote once we know the cause.

    That light is the valve's diagnostic code. The flash pattern lines up with a chart printed on the heater's label. Common codes point to a weak thermopile, an open thermal switch or a sensor fault, and we test the part the code points to before replacing the valve. Counting the flashes helps us bring the right part.

    A weak thermocouple is the usual cause. A plugged pilot orifice can also make the flame too small to heat it. On vapor-resistant tanks, a lint-clogged flame arrestor can starve the burner and trip the thermal switch, which looks like a pilot outage from the outside. Garage drafts can snuff out an older pilot.

    A thermocouple is a small copper-sheathed rod that sits in the pilot flame and makes a tiny electric current. The current keeps a magnet in the gas valve pulled open. If the flame dies, the current stops. When the pilot goes out, the magnet lets go and the valve shuts off gas to both the pilot and the burner, which is the safety that keeps raw gas from filling a closet. Weak thermocouples cause more pilot outages than anything else.

    Flush the tank once a year. Draining a few gallons from the bottom valve every few months helps too. GNV Plumbing can do all of that in one visit. With hard water, flush more often.
