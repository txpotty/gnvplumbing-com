    Grease Trap Inspection and Repair in Wilmington

    An empty trap is the only time anyone can see what is wrong with it. We look for cracked baffles and missing outlet tees. A missing outlet tee lets the floating grease cap pour straight into the sewer, and it is one of the most common failures we find on older concrete interceptors. Small fixes can often happen the same visit.

    Grease Trap Pumping and Hauling in Wilmington

    Trap waste is regulated as a liquid waste, so in most places it has to be hauled by a licensed hauler to an approved receiving facility, not dumped down a manhole or into a dumpster. We do both the pumping and the hauling. Our vacuum trucks pull the full contents of the trap into a sealed tank, and the load gets logged with gallons and destination. No barrels left behind.

    Hydro-Jetting for Grease Lines in Wilmington, DE

    A cable machine pokes a hole through grease. Jetting actually removes it. Our jetters run around 3,000 to 4,000 psi through a spinning nozzle, which scours the whole inside of the pipe and pushes the loosened grease downstream. Afterward a camera pass confirms the pipe is clean.

    Compliance Documentation for FOG Programs in Wilmington, DE

    Inspectors usually ask for at least a year of pumping records. A clean trap with no paperwork can still get written up. Each sheet shows the date, gallons, measured depths and hauler name, and copies can go by email to your manager or the corporate office after each visit. The originals stay on site.

    Outdoor Grease Interceptor Pumping in Wilmington, DE

    In-ground interceptors need a vacuum truck and usually a pressure wand to break up the cap. Every compartment gets opened and pumped to the bottom. While the tank is empty, we look at the inlet and outlet tees, the baffle wall, the risers and the lid seals, because a cracked riser lets groundwater and dirt in. Lids go back on tight.

    Under-Sink Grease Trap Maintenance in Wilmington, DE

    Under-sink hydromechanical traps are rated by flow, usually 10 to 50 gallons per minute, and they hold far less than people expect. Busy kitchens need them cleaned every week or two. We take the lid off, remove grease and solids, scrape the baffles, check the flow control fitting on the inlet, and replace the lid gasket if it is flattened or torn. Most odor from these units comes from a bad gasket.
