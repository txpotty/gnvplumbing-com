    Underground Pipe Damage: Common Causes and Modern Repair Solutions

    By Luis Ortega · August 31, 2026 · 6 min read

    The pipes you never see do some of the hardest work in the house. The water service line runs from the meter to the house, usually 3/4 or 1 inch pipe buried below the frost line. A sewer lateral carries everything from the drains out to the city main or the septic tank. Both sit several feet underground, and either one can fail with very little warning. These days a repair rarely means digging up the lawn from one end to the other.

    What Damages Buried Pipes in Delaware

    Tree roots cause more sewer trouble than anything else. Fast-growing shade trees like willows and silver maples send roots a long way looking for water, and a lot of ornamentals do the same. A hairline crack or loose joint leaks warm, nutrient-rich vapor, and roots find that vapor and work their way inside. Inside, they grow into a mat that snags waste and can eventually split the pipe.

    Sandy soil drains quickly but can shift and settle, which may stress buried sewer lines and water service pipes over time.

    Soil movement does damage slowly. Clay that swells after rain and shrinks in a drought keeps working against buried rock and hard layers that do not move at all. Over years that cycle shifts joints and cracks rigid pipe, and it leaves low spots called bellies where waste settles. Heavy trucks parked over a shallow line and poor bedding during construction make it worse.

    Pipe material matters as well. Older neighborhoods may still have clay tile or cast iron sewer lines. Some houses also have galvanized steel water service lines that rust from the inside out. ABS and PVC, standard from the 1970s onward, will not rust, though they still crack and separate at the joints.

    Rapid growth in coastal and suburban areas means many newer homes in Delaware are built with PEX supply lines and PVC drains.

    Warning Signs of Underground Pipe Damage

    Buried pipe problems show up in roundabout ways. Watch for these:

    • Repeat backups in more than one fixture, starting with whatever drain sits lowest.
    • A gurgling toilet when the washing machine drains.
    • Sewer smell in the yard or around a cleanout.
    • A green or soggy strip of lawn that stands out in a dry spell.
    • Sinkholes or depressions along the path of the line.
    • A water meter that keeps moving with every fixture off, pointing to a service line leak.
    • Low water pressure through the whole house.

    Modern Repair Methods

    Diagnosis comes first. On the sewer side, a waterproof camera goes in at the cleanout and records every foot of pipe out to the main. The footage shows roots, cracks, offsets and bellies, and a locator at the camera head marks the depth and location of each one from the surface. On a water service line, we pressure test it and listen with acoustic gear to find the leak.

    Cured-in-place lining slides a resin-soaked felt liner into the old sewer, inflates it and lets it cure into a new jointless pipe inside the old one. On a 4 inch lateral, the cured liner takes up very little of the inside diameter, and it flows better than rough cast iron did. Cracks and root entry points get sealed over, and most jobs need nothing more than the cleanout for access. Pipe bursting pulls new HDPE through the line while a cone-shaped head breaks the old pipe outward. Bursting suits pipe too broken to line. Spot repair means digging up one section to swap out one bad joint or a single sag. For water service, directional boring runs a new line from meter to house with a small pit at each end.

    Cost comes down to footage and depth. A camera inspection typically runs $150 to $400. Spot sewer repairs often land between $1,500 and $4,500, trenchless lining or bursting of a full residential lateral commonly runs about $4,000 to $12,000, and a new water service line is roughly $1,500 to $5,000. Trenchless is not always cheaper per foot than an excavator, but you are not paying to rebuild the driveway and the pavers afterward.

    When to Call a Professional

    Drains that keep backing up, or a meter showing a leak you cannot find, call for a professional inspection. Underground work often needs permits and utility locates, and careful digging around gas lines.
