    Why a Sewer Camera Inspection Belongs on Every Delaware Home Inspection

    By Andrew Blake · August 13, 2026 · 5 min read

    A general home inspection covers a lot. The inspector checks the roof, the electrical panel, the HVAC and the water heater, along with whatever plumbing is exposed. What most of them do not do is look inside the main sewer line that runs from the house to the street or septic tank. Running water in every fixture for 20 minutes proves very little, because a line can drain normally for a short test and still be cracked, sagging or full of roots. A sewer scope is one of the best add-ons a buyer can order before closing.

    What a Sewer Camera Inspection Involves

    The plumber feeds a waterproof camera on a flexible push cable through a cleanout, usually near the foundation or out in the front yard. As the camera travels the line, it records video and shows it live on a monitor. A transmitter at the camera head lets us mark the spot and depth of any problem from the surface. Plan on 30 to 60 minutes. Ask for the video file and a written summary before the plumber leaves.

    No cleanout? We can sometimes go in from a roof vent or through a toilet flange. That access may add to the price. Most residential scopes run $150 to $350 when there is a cleanout to work from.

    Before the visit, ask the listing agent for any records of past sewer work. Invoices for root cutting or paperwork from a previous lining job tell us a lot. With that history in hand, we know where to slow the camera down and can check whether the old repairs are still holding. Sellers often have it in a drawer.

    Ask the seller, too, whether the house has ever had a main line backup into the basement or the lowest shower. If the answer is yes, we slow down near the foundation, where most of those backups start. Honest answers save time.

    What the Camera Can Reveal

    Sewer problems are expensive, and you rarely see them from inside the house. A camera can turn up:

    • Root intrusion at the joints, most often near big, older trees.
    • Bellies or sags where the pipe settled and waste collects.
    • Offset or separated joints from years of soil movement.
    • Cracked or collapsed sections in clay tile or cast iron.
    • Heavy corrosion and scale inside older cast iron.
    • Grease buildup that points to ongoing maintenance.
    • Bad past repairs, like two different pipe materials joined without the right coupling.

    Why It Matters for Delaware Buyers

    Some homes from the late 1970s through the mid 1990s contain polybutylene pipe, which is best replaced before it fails.

    Every era of construction brings its own sewer risks. In older parts of town, the original clay or cast iron is often still in service decades past its expected life. A lot of houses from the 1960s through the 1980s have cast iron under the slab that rots from the inside. Newer ABS and PVC is not immune either. Expansive soil and poor bedding still cause bellies and pulled joints.

    Trees add another wrinkle. Buyers love mature shade trees, and so do sewer lines, because the roots follow moisture straight to the joints. Out past the sewer district, many properties are on septic. There the camera shows the condition of the line out to the tank, and that footage goes nicely with a separate septic inspection.

    Pumping the tank on a routine schedule and limiting water use during wet seasons helps a septic system last.

    How the Results Help You Negotiate

    Sewer repairs cost real money. A spot repair can run $1,500 to $4,500, while a new lateral may cost $4,000 to $12,000 or more. Finding a problem before closing gives you options. You can ask the seller to fix it or ask for a credit, or walk away. If the line looks good, you get a baseline video to compare against later. Sellers benefit too, since a pre-listing scope lets them fix problems on their own schedule.

    When to Call a Professional

    Book the scope early in your inspection contingency period. That leaves time to get repair quotes if the video shows trouble. A licensed plumber, not a general inspector, is the right person for this, because we can read the footage, pinpoint each defect and price the real fix.
