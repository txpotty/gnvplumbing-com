    How to Protect Your Pipes Before the Next Delaware Freeze

    By Andrew Blake · October 6, 2026 · 5 min read

    Hard freezes are usually short here, but a single cold night can still split an outdoor faucet left connected to a hose.

    Pipes do not need a long winter to burst. Plenty of places with mild weather still get a night or two each year below freezing for six hours or more. The houses that get hurt are the ones built as if it never freezes. Supply lines run in exterior walls, the backflow preventer sits bare in the yard, and the pool pump has no cover at all. One night like that can split a pipe that has been fine for twenty years.

    Why Frozen Pipes Burst

    Water expands about nine percent as it freezes. Oddly enough, the ice is not what does the damage. When the plug forms, it shoves the water ahead of it toward a closed faucet or valve, and pressure builds in that trapped section until the copper splits or a brass valve body cracks. The split usually stays sealed while the line is frozen. So the leak shows up the next morning, when the sun hits the pipe and water starts spraying.

    Above-ground pipe is the weak spot in most houses. Exposed copper and PVC shed heat quickly, especially on clear, still nights when the temperature drops hard after sunset. Wind across an open yard pulls heat away even faster. A backflow preventer sitting in the open on a windy night can freeze solid at temperatures that a pipe inside a wall would never notice.

    The Parts of a House Most at Risk

    Before the forecast drops, walk the outside of the house with a flashlight and check these spots:

    • Backflow preventers on irrigation and fire lines. The brass body sits above ground and costs real money to replace when it splits.
    • Hose bibs on exterior walls, particularly the old compression style that is not frost-free.
    • Pool and spa equipment, meaning the pump, filter, heater and the PVC between them.
    • Irrigation risers and drip manifolds sitting out in the open in the planting beds.
    • Evaporative cooler supply lines that climb the outside wall to the roof, on houses that have them.
    • Water heaters and supply pipe in unheated garages, mostly in colder areas.
    • The main supply where it comes up out of the ground near the meter or pressure regulator.

    Cheap Steps That Prevent Damage

    Most freeze protection costs less than a service call. Slide foam pipe sleeves over every exposed run and tape the seams so cold air cannot get in. Pull the hose off each hose bib and fit an insulated cover, because a connected hose traps water in the faucet body. For a backflow preventer, use an insulated pouch, or wrap it in old towels and set a bucket over it. Low-wattage heat tape rated for outdoor plumbing is money well spent where hard freezes are common.

    Check the pool controller for a freeze protection setting. On most newer controllers it kicks the pump on once the air sensor reads a few degrees above freezing. During a hard freeze, let a faucet on an outside wall drip overnight, so pressure has somewhere to go and the line never sits still. Open the sink cabinets on exterior walls. The point is to let warm room air reach the supply lines behind the cabinet back.

    A humid climate with hot summers and cool, damp winters puts steady stress on crawlspace plumbing and water heaters in Delaware.

    Colder climates call for permanent fixes. A frost-free sillcock moves the shutoff 6 to 12 inches inside the house, where the wall stays warm. Insulated crawlspaces and heat tape on the runs that froze last year handle most of what is left. If you leave a cabin or second home empty for the winter, shut off the main, open the low drains and blow the lines out, because a dry pipe has nothing in it to freeze.

    If a Pipe Freezes

    A trickle from the kitchen faucet on a cold morning usually means a frozen line. Leave that faucet open. Work along the pipe with a hair dryer, starting near the faucet and moving toward the cold section, or set a space heater a safe distance away. Never use a torch or any open flame. Have the main shutoff located first, because a split pipe leaks the minute the ice lets go. A split hose bib or one section of pipe usually runs $200 to $500 to repair, and a cracked backflow preventer can be $400 to $900 or more installed.
