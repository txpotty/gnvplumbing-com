    Tankless vs. Tank Water Heaters: What Works Best in Delaware

    By Travis Baines · September 18, 2026 · 6 min read

    When the old heater lets go, the first question is always the same. Do you put in another tank, or is this the time to go tankless? National buying guides skip the local conditions, and those details matter more than anything on the box. Incoming water temperature, how hot or cold the garage runs and how much mineral is in the water change the answer, sometimes by a lot, so it pays to know them before you sign anything.

    Salt air in coastal parts of Delaware speeds corrosion on outdoor fittings, hose bibs, and exposed metal pipe.

    How Each Type Works

    A tank heater stores 40 to 75 gallons and keeps it hot around the clock with a gas burner or electric elements. They are familiar and inexpensive to put in. The tradeoff is standby loss, the energy spent reheating water nobody is using. Drain it and you wait.

    A tankless unit heats water only when a fixture calls for it. Open a tap and a flow sensor lights the burner, the water picks up heat in the exchanger, and it keeps coming until you shut it off. Capacity is rated in gallons per minute at a given temperature rise. There is no tank size to compare. Most whole-house units burn gas, because an electric tankless for a full house often needs more amperage than the panel can spare.

    How Local Conditions Change the Math

    Tankless output depends on how many degrees the heater has to add. With 70 degree water coming in, a 199,000 BTU unit raising it to 120 degrees can run three or four fixtures at the same time. In warm climates the incoming water stays mild even in winter. Less temperature rise gives you more flow. Where the supply water is cold most of the year, sizing gets tighter and matters more.

    Garage temperature counts too. A tank in a hot garage loses less heat to standby, which narrows the tankless efficiency edge in summer. Those same hot garages make hybrid heat pump water heaters work very well, since they pull heat out of the surrounding air and cool the garage a little as a side benefit. Put one in a cold basement and it works much harder.

    Hard water hits tankless units hardest. Scale builds fast on the heat exchanger, and most manufacturers require yearly descaling to keep the warranty. Tank heaters suffer as well, with sediment cutting years off their life and making that popping noise you hear when the burner fires. A softener or scale inhibitor ahead of the heater pays off either way.

    Water in Delaware ranges from moderately soft to hard depending on the source, and groundwater areas often see more scale on fixtures.

    Comparing the Costs

    Prices move with fuel type, venting and code upgrades. Here are typical installed ranges:

    • Standard gas or electric tank, 40 to 50 gallons: about $1,400 to $2,800 installed.
    • Whole-house gas tankless: about $3,000 to $6,000 installed, more if the gas line has to be upsized.
    • Hybrid heat pump tank: about $2,800 to $5,000 installed, sometimes offset by utility rebates or federal tax credits.
    • Yearly tankless descaling: about $150 to $300 if a plumber does it.
    • Tank flush: about $100 to $200, once a year in hard water.

    The gas line is the cost people miss. A whole-house tankless can draw 180,000 BTU or more, and lines sized for a 40,000 BTU tank are often too small. Switching from tank to tankless usually also means new stainless or PVC venting, plus a condensate line if the unit is a condensing model, and a 120 volt outlet for the control board. Ask to see them on the quote line by line.

    Which One Should You Choose?

    Budget replacements and rentals still call for a tank. It also fits houses where the gas line and venting are not set up for tankless. Tankless is a good fit for bigger families who run out of hot water and for owners planning to stay, and for anyone who wants that corner of the garage back. A hybrid heat pump deserves a hard look in an all-electric house with a garage install. Expect a good tank to last 8 to 12 years on hard water and a maintained tankless 15 to 20.

    When to Call a Professional

    Water heater installs touch gas piping, venting and combustion air, along with the expansion tank and the T and P relief line. Every one of those gets checked at inspection.
