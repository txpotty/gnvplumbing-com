    Everything in the trap comes out, from the floating grease cap down to the sludge of food solids on the floor. Haulers call it brown grease. Yellow grease, the used fryer oil in your collection bin, is a separate product that gets recycled and should never go into the trap.

    It goes to a permitted receiving facility. Some facilities process it for biofuel or digesters. The rest is dewatered and disposed of under the facility's permit, and every load we haul is logged so the trail runs from your trap to the gate.

    Rules come from your sewer district or city pretreatment program. Most follow some version of the 25 percent rule. Plenty also cap the interval, want a year or more of records on site, and ban enzymes in place of pumping, so we look up your program before we schedule.

    Staff can clean a small under-sink trap between pump-outs. The waste still needs legal disposal. Many kitchens have staff skim daily and book us to fully pump and scrape every few weeks, which keeps the interval long without skipping the records the inspector expects.

    The terms get mixed up constantly. Generally a trap is the small unit indoors, often under 100 gallons. Interceptor usually means the large tank in the ground outside, often 500 gallons and up, and codes size the two in different ways.

    FOG stands for fats, oils and grease. It pours as a liquid and sets up hard once it cools. In a sewer pipe it sticks to the walls in layers, catches food scraps and wipes, and eventually closes the pipe down to a pencil-sized opening.
